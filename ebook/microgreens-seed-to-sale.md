# Introduction: Why Microgreens?

Microgreens are young edible plants harvested after the cotyledons open and,
for some crops, just as the first true leaf develops. They are not sprouts:
sprouts are eaten with their seed and root, while microgreens are grown in a
sanitary medium, cut above that medium, and sold as a fresh crop. This small
distinction creates a useful business opportunity. A tray can move from seed
to harvest in roughly one to three weeks, a shelf can multiply a room's
production area, and a grower can plant to an actual standing order instead
of gambling on a season-long field crop.

The attractive headline—fast crops and premium prices—is not a promise of
easy money. Profitable trays come from consistent germination, clean handling,
accurate records, and dependable customers. Treat this book as a starting
standard operating procedure. Begin with a few forgiving crops, sell only
what you can grow and cool reliably, and change one variable at a time. Local
food rules and buyers' requirements always outrank generalized guidance here.

## A practical first goal

Choose three crops (for example radish, pea, and sunflower), one outlet, and
two harvest days. Grow a small trial, weigh both seed and harvest, let a few
buyers taste it, then repeat the crops that sell. Your first crop plan should
produce information as well as greens: germination percentage, days, yield,
labor minutes, customer feedback, and delivered price.

# Understanding Microgreens: Science & Nutrition

The cotyledons are the seed leaves that fuel early growth; true leaves are
the plant's later, species-specific leaves. Flavor, color, texture, and
harvest stage differ dramatically among species. Brassicas such as radish,
broccoli, kale, cabbage, kohlrabi, arugula, and mustard are fast and
uniform. Large-seeded peas and sunflower make a substantial, crunchy crop.
Herbs and alliums take longer, use less seed, and earn their price through
aroma and scarcity.

Microgreens can contribute vitamins, carotenoids, phenolics, and other
phytonutrients, but nutrient concentration varies by cultivar, light,
medium, age, and analysis method. Avoid saying that every tray is “40 times
more nutritious” than a mature vegetable: the widely cited University of
Maryland/USDA comparison reported ranges for selected nutrients in selected
microgreens, not a universal health claim. Sell flavor, freshness, and
culinary versatility; make only claims you can substantiate.

Food safety begins at seed. Use untreated seed intended for edible production,
keep supplier lot information, use potable water, clean and sanitize food
contact surfaces, exclude pets and pests, and keep harvest and packing areas
separate from dirty tray washing. People who are pregnant, elderly,
immunocompromised, or serving such customers should consult current public
health guidance and use especially conservative sanitation practices.

# Setting Up Your Grow Space

A spare room, basement, insulated garage, or small commercial room can work
when it is cleanable, dry, and supplied with potable water and safe
electricity. Plan the movement of work in one direction: dry seed and clean
trays → sowing → blackout → lighted production → harvest → cold storage.
This reduces cross-contamination and wasted walking.

Aim for 65–75°F (18–24°C) for most crops, 40–60% relative humidity, gentle
continuous air movement, and 12–16 hours of light. A timer prevents skipped
days. Put GFCI-protected electrical equipment above possible spills, make no
overloaded power-strip chains, and leave access around racks for cleaning.
Use a thermometer/hygrometer at crop height; a cheap sensor can reveal hot
top shelves or a humid corner before mold does.

Start with one wire rack and four usable levels. Put blackout stacks low,
lighted crops in the middle, and dry supplies off the floor. Leave enough
vertical clearance to raise lights and harvest without crushing the canopy.
Protect walls and floors from water, collect runoff, and never assume a
residential space meets local commercial-food requirements.

## Commissioning a new room

Before sowing saleable seed, run a seven-day dry and wet commissioning test.
Place a thermometer/hygrometer on the warmest and coolest shelves, run the
lights and fan on their intended timers, and record temperature and humidity
morning and evening. Fill several trays with water to practice carrying,
bottom watering, drainage, and cleanup. Check that a tripped circuit does not
leave a wet floor, that lights are securely supported, and that the cooler
holds its target temperature under a realistic load. Solve drainage and
electrical problems before introducing food.

Make cleaning visible. Keep a written schedule for trays, racks, sinks,
knives, scales, cooler handles, and floors. Cleaning removes soil and organic
matter; sanitizing follows cleaning and uses a food-contact sanitizer at its
label concentration and contact time. Let surfaces air dry where appropriate.
Mixing chemicals, using unlabelled spray bottles, or treating sanitation as a
once-a-month task creates avoidable risk.

## A sensible purchasing sequence

Buy fewer, stronger trays before buying automation. A cracked tray is a
sanitation and labor problem, while an oversized rack creates pressure to
grow unsold crops. Choose lights with a documented electrical rating and
mounting method, calculate the circuit load, and use a timer rated for that
load. Purchase packaging only after measuring your actual harvested volume;
containers that look attractive but crush a canopy or leave excessive
headspace waste product and money. Keep invoices and manuals in a single
business folder for warranty, accounting, and inspection purposes.

# Choosing Seeds & Varieties

Choose crops from customer demand first, then match the crop to your room.
Radish and pea are generally forgiving. Sunflower can be lucrative but needs
good seed, rinsing, weight, and hull management. Basil, cilantro, amaranth,
fennel, and alliums reward precision and should follow a successful basic
system. Buy a test quantity, retain the label, record its lot, and test one
tray before committing to a restaurant menu.

The densities in this book are **starting trials**, not fixed laws. A
shallower medium, a different cultivar, compact climate, or longer harvest
window changes density. Over-seeding creates weak stems, trapped moisture,
and mold; under-seeding wastes tray area. Record dry ounces, sow date,
blackout release, harvest weight, and customer grade for every trial.

## Seed receiving and storage

On arrival, write the supplier, lot, variety, date received, and any
germination information on a container or inventory sheet. Keep seed cool,
dry, dark, sealed against pests, and away from cleaning chemicals. Use
first-in, first-out inventory while retaining enough lot identification to
trace a sold batch. A small germination trial—one labeled tray grown exactly
as you intend to sell it—is cheaper than losing a week of restaurant orders.
Do not blend old and new lots until both have passed a trial.

# Planting Methods

For soil or coir, fill a holed 10×20 tray with ¾–1 inch of evenly moist
medium. It should clump when squeezed but not drip. Level it; uneven media
makes uneven canopy height. Weigh dry seed, rinse and soak only where the
chart calls for it, drain well, and distribute in a single, even layer.
Press seed gently into the surface—do not bury most microgreen seed. Mist
only enough to settle it, cover, label crop/lot/date/density, and move it to
blackout.

For mats, pre-moisten the mat as directed, drain it, then distribute seed
evenly. Mats simplify harvest but may need closer irrigation observation.
For all methods, use a clean scoop for each seed lot and sanitize trays
between crops. Never reuse moldy medium for edible crops.

# Germination (Blackout Phase)

Blackout is a controlled germination period, not a reason to forget trays.
Nest covered trays or stack with a second tray and modest, even weight when
the crop benefits from pressure. Check daily: seeds need moisture and oxygen,
not standing water. Condensation, a sour smell, or fuzzy growth across the
seed layer calls for immediate inspection, airflow adjustment, and often
discarding the affected tray.

Release crops when roots have anchored and shoots are pushing the cover, at
the days shown in the chart. Large seeds such as sunflower and peas commonly
benefit from a weighted stack; fine herb seeds usually need a lid without
heavy pressure. Briefly expose only when the crop is ready—stretching a crop
in darkness weakens it.

# Bottom Watering Technique

Set the holed crop tray inside a solid tray. Add a shallow layer of potable
water to the outer tray and allow the medium to wick it upward. Check whether
the medium has become evenly moist, then drain standing water rather than
leaving roots submerged. Frequency depends on crop size, light, room
conditions, and medium; a mature sunflower tray may drink far more than a
new broccoli tray.

Lift a tray rather than watering by habit. It should feel lighter before
watering but never bone dry. Bottom watering keeps foliage drier and reduces
soil splash, yet it is not a cure for poor sanitation or crowding. Clean the
reservoir tray after every crop; algae or biofilm means the process is too wet
or cleaning is incomplete.

# Light & Growth Phase

Move released trays under full-spectrum LEDs or efficient shop lights. Begin
with lights roughly 8–12 inches above the canopy, then adjust for the fixture:
bleached tips suggest excess intensity or heat; stretching suggests too little
light or too much distance. Provide 12–16 hours on a timer and dark rest
daily. Keep leaves dry, maintain modest airflow, rotate trays only if shelf
conditions differ, and record light height and response.

Most short-cycle brassicas do not require fertilizer in a suitable medium.
Longer crops may respond differently; if you use nutrients, use a
food-crop-appropriate product, follow its label and local rules, and validate
the crop before selling it. More fertilizer cannot repair bad seed or bad
climate.

# Harvesting

Harvest to an order whenever possible. Stop irrigation early enough for the
canopy to dry, then cut with a clean, sharp knife just above medium level.
Avoid pulling roots or dragging the blade through medium. Inspect color,
odor, debris, and damaged leaves as you cut. Keep bins shaded and cold; warm
harvests lose shelf life quickly.

Weigh a representative tray and packed units. Yield per tray and packed
weight are different measurements—trim loss, hulls, and moisture matter.
Record both. Do not sell a tray with unexplained mold, off odor, pest damage,
or contamination; discard it and identify the process cause.

## Harvest-day checklist

1. Verify the order list, variety, package size, and delivery route.
2. Wash hands; clean and sanitize the harvest table, knife, scale, and tubs.
3. Check each tray for quality and confirm the canopy is dry.
4. Cut, inspect, weigh, and pack one variety at a time to prevent mix-ups.
5. Apply the correct product and batch label before moving to the next crop.
6. Cool packed product promptly; log cooler temperature and finished quantity.
7. Reconcile packed inventory to invoices, then clean tools and record waste.

This deliberate order makes traceability practical. It also makes it possible
to notice a short crop early enough to contact a buyer, substitute an agreed
mix, or issue a credit rather than surprise them at delivery.

# Post-Harvest Handling & Storage

Use clean, sanitized food-contact tools and dry packaging. Do not routinely
wash a clean crop: added water shortens shelf life unless it is removed
thoroughly. If washing is required by the product or customer, use potable
water and a validated drying process. Pack gently into labeled clamshells or
bags, cool promptly, and hold at approximately 34–40°F (1–4°C) without
freezing. Validate shelf life in your packaging and refrigerator rather than
assuming a universal number; seven to ten days is a common goal for robust
crops, not a guarantee.

Labels should meet applicable law and typically include product identity, net
weight, farm/business contact information, harvest/packed date or code, and
any required handling statement. Maintain a simple one-step-back/one-step-
forward log: seed lot and crop batch, harvest date, customer, quantity, and
delivery date.

## Shelf-life validation

For each new crop and package, keep three representative packs at your normal
refrigerated holding temperature. Inspect daily for condensation, wilt, odor,
decay, and color loss. Record the first day quality becomes unacceptable.
Repeat after any material, cooler, harvest, or wash-process change. Date
labels should be based on this evidence and local requirements, not a
competitor's claim. Rotate finished product first-in, first-out and protect
it from raw foods, chemicals, and door-temperature swings.

# Pest, Mold & Disease Management

| Symptom | Likely contributors | First response |
|---|---|---|
| Root hairs mistaken for mold | normal root hairs; often disappear when moistened | Confirm before discarding; root hairs are localized at roots, not webbing across seed. |
| White webbing or sour odor | excess water, poor airflow, contaminated seed/media | Isolate and discard unsafe crop; clean area, reduce density/wetness. |
| Damping-off | saturated medium, heat, pathogen pressure | Discard affected crop; sanitize trays and revise watering/airflow. |
| Fungus gnats | wet organic medium, debris | Dry surface appropriately, clean drains, use traps to monitor. |
| Uneven canopy | uneven density, media, light, or moisture | Calibrate seed weight/spreading and level the tray. |

Prevention is cheaper than rescue: start clean, control water, use airflow,
separate dirty and clean work, and inspect daily. Do not rely on unapproved
chemical treatments for a food crop. If recurring losses occur, pause sales
of that crop, save records, seek local extension or food-safety advice, and
rebuild the procedure.

## Crop notes and harvest cues

### Sunflower (black oil)
Rinse after soaking, sow in a single layer, and use a second tray with even
weight through germination. Release before stems bend under the cover. Pick
off hulls during light growth; cut above the medium when cotyledons are open
and leaves are crisp. Reject seed with persistent off odor or mold.

### Pea shoots (speckled/dun)
Soak, drain thoroughly, and weight lightly for strong roots. Harvest tall,
bright shoots above the first node before tendrils become tough. Peas drink
heavily under lights, so check tray weight often and avoid a permanent water
reservoir.

### Radish (daikon and China rose)
Do not soak. Spread the tiny seeds exactly; crowding creates thin, wet
stems. Daikon is a reliable green workhorse, while China rose earns value
from its pink stem. Harvest at open cotyledons while pungency is fresh.

### Broccoli
Use clean, untreated seed and modest density. Broccoli is mild and suited to
mixes, but its fine canopy shows excess moisture quickly. Cut at open
cotyledons, before it elongates or yellows.

### Arugula
Sow lightly and expect slower, less uniform germination than radish. Keep
the crop cool enough to retain quality. Cut when cotyledons are full and
peppery, before crowded stems weaken.

### Kale
Kale tolerates a beginner's learning curve. Keep density even for a clean
canopy and harvest with broad cotyledons. Its mild flavor makes it useful in
a salad mix rather than as a dramatic garnish.

### Red cabbage
Use the same disciplined brassica process, but protect its attractive purple
stem from excess heat and poor light. Harvest at full cotyledons for crunch
and color; pack gently to prevent bruising.

### Purple kohlrabi
Kohlrabi is a colorful, mild brassica. It needs no soak or special feeding;
uniform spreading, bottom watering, and timely harvest deliver the premium
appearance chefs buy.

### Amaranth
Use very little seed and a fine, level medium. This warm-loving, delicate red
crop is easily overwatered and damaged at harvest. Cut only dry, upright
canopies and sell quickly in small premium packs.

### Cilantro
Crack or soak only if your seed supplier's guidance supports it; drain well.
Germination is slow and irregular, so do not judge too early. Harvest when
the first true leaves provide the unmistakable aroma; price its labor.

### Basil
Basil needs warmth, clean handling, and patience. Do not soak its fine seed.
Avoid chilling injury: cool promptly after harvest without storing it
unnecessarily cold for long periods. Cut tender, aromatic cotyledon-to-true-
leaf stage greens with a very clean blade.

### Beet
Soak briefly, rinse, and sow evenly; multigerm seed can produce clustered
shoots. Keep medium moist, not saturated, and harvest bright stems when
cotyledons are open. Trial cultivars for color and hull behavior.

### Swiss chard
Treat chard like beet but expect a slightly faster, sturdier crop. Soak and
drain, apply modest weight, and harvest colorful stems before the canopy
becomes coarse. It performs well in visual mixes.

### Mustard (red and wasabi)
Mustard is fast, spicy, and highly marketable in small amounts. Do not soak.
Use airflow and conservative density because its quick canopy traps moisture.
Harvest at open cotyledons and label heat level accurately for customers.

### Wheatgrass
Soak, rinse, and spread a dense but even layer. Cut clean blades when they
are bright green and upright, generally before a second cut is contemplated.
For food-safety simplicity, sell first-cut product and clean thoroughly
between batches.

### Chia
Chia becomes gelatinous when wet, so never pre-soak. Sow a thin, even layer
onto fully moistened medium, cover gently, and avoid over-misting. Harvest
the tender canopy dry, before it mats or collapses.

### Fenugreek
Soak and rinse, then sow with enough spacing for airflow. Expect a distinctive
maple-like aroma that can surprise customers; offer a tasting sample. Cut
when cotyledons are broad and crisp, not yellowed.

### Cress
Cress seed is mucilaginous: do not soak and do not flood it. Use light,
even moisture and a level surface. Its sharp flavor supports garnish packs,
and its delicate stems demand a gentle dry harvest.

### Fennel
Fennel is slow, fine, and premium. Use a light hand with seed and water,
provide steady warmth and light, and harvest only bright, feathery shoots
with a clear anise scent. Schedule it to a chef order, not speculative volume.

### Onion/chives
Alliums germinate slowly and benefit from patience, stable warmth, and
consistent moisture. Do not bury seed deeply. Cut upright, narrow leaves
above medium when onion aroma is developed; avoid crushing the canopy.

### Popcorn shoots
Use untreated popcorn specifically sold for edible shoots. Soak, drain, and
weight to anchor kernels. Some growers harvest pale sweet shoots after
blackout; this book's lighted method produces greener shoots. State the style
clearly and never sell bitter or damaged shoots.

### Buckwheat lettuce
Soak briefly and rinse well. Buckwheat hulls are a major quality issue:
release at the right time, gently brush or lift loose hulls, and avoid packing
them with the crop. Do not use untreated seed of uncertain edible status.

# Business Planning & Legal Basics

Pick a business structure with an accountant or small-business adviser, then
register names, obtain tax registrations, and separate business banking.
Before a single sale, ask the local health department, agriculture department,
market manager, zoning office, and insurer what applies to microgreens grown
and packed in your location. Cottage-food exemptions, permits, refrigeration,
labeling, water testing, inspected facilities, sales tax, and delivery rules
vary substantially. Restaurant buyers may require insurance and a supplier
agreement even where direct sales are exempt.

Create simple written procedures for cleaning, batch records, lot traceability,
recall contact, cooler temperature, and complaint handling. These documents
build buyer confidence and make a small operation easier to scale.

# Pricing, Packaging & Branding

Price from cost and margin, not competitor fear. For each product, include
seed, medium, tray wear, packaging, labels, labor, delivery, market fees,
spoilage, and a share of utilities and insurance. Then set a wholesale price
that supports reliable recurring volume and a retail price that supports
sampling, market time, and unsold-product risk. A 2-ounce chef garnish pack,
a 4-ounce retail clamshell, and a pound bulk bag should not have the same
per-pound price.

Use dry, food-appropriate packaging sized to prevent crushing. A simple brand
needs a readable farm name, crop, net weight, packed/harvest date or code,
storage direction, and contact channel. Make a promise you can keep:
harvested to order, local weekly delivery, living trays, or a consistent chef
mix. Photograph products honestly and obtain permission before naming buyers.

# Sales Channels & Marketing

Restaurants value consistency, flavor, and delivery reliability. Bring a
labeled sample with a one-page availability list, price, harvest day, storage
note, and a low-friction first order. Farmers markets provide immediate
feedback but require sampling discipline, weather planning, and enough
inventory variety. CSA subscribers value predictable add-ons; grocery co-ops
often demand barcodes, insurance, invoicing, and steady cold chain.

Schedule marketing with production. Every Monday, publish availability; every
Tuesday, confirm orders; plant against the next forecast; harvest as late as
possible before delivery; and ask one customer weekly for a specific quality
observation. Track leads, samples, conversion, average order, repeat rate,
and late deliveries. A social post is useful when it leads to an order form,
email list, or conversation—not merely likes.

## Sales conversation and service standard

Ask a chef what they use now, which flavors are missing, what pack size fits
their station, and which delivery window is realistic. Offer a small,
specified trial rather than an open-ended free sample: crop, weight, price
after the trial, harvest day, and storage expectation. Send a short message
the day before delivery to confirm quantities. Invoice consistently, publish
a clear minimum order or delivery fee, and decide in advance how credits,
late changes, and cancellations work.

For direct customers, teach one use at a time: fold pea shoots into a
sandwich, finish eggs with radish, or add a mild brassica mix after cooking.
Recipe cards and tasting notes make a premium product less intimidating.
Capture consent before subscribing people to messages. A small email list of
repeat buyers is more valuable than a large audience with no delivery area.

# Scaling Up: From Hobby to Full Operation

Scale the process before the rack count. Standardize tray type, label format,
crop codes, densities, blackout location, harvest tools, and cleaning steps.
When a crop is predictable, add one shelf or one customer group at a time.
Measure output as sellable ounces per tray, labor minutes per tray, and
on-time order percentage; a full rack of inconsistent product is not scale.

At 20–40 trays a week, plan delivery routes, backup lights, seed reorder
points, cooler capacity, written training, and a dedicated wash/pack zone.
Do not add employees or lease space based on a single market weekend. Use
recurring orders and a cash reserve to fund each capacity step.

## When to add capacity

Add capacity only when three conditions are true: at least six to eight weeks
of repeat demand supports it, the current workflow is documented and meeting
quality targets, and cash covers the purchase plus a crop-loss reserve. A
simple capacity calculation is:

`weekly trays required = weekly sellable ounces / average sellable ounces per tray`.

Then add a loss allowance—often 10–20% during early growth—and round up only
to a manageable planting batch. Review the calculation by crop; a tray of
sunflower, basil, and radish are not interchangeable. Keep a small buffer for
standing orders, but do not fill every shelf with speculative inventory.

## Labor, quality, and waste controls

Time the full batch, not just the pleasant part of growing. Include tray
washing, seed measuring, sowing, moving stacks, watering, harvest, labeling,
delivery, customer messages, bookkeeping, and cleanup. Divide weekly labor
minutes by sellable ounces to discover which products are truly expensive.
Premium herbs may justify careful work; a low-priced crop with repeated hull
removal may not. Improve a bottleneck with a standardized scoop, a better
label printer, or a better tray before adding an employee.

Sort waste into crop loss, trim, packaging damage, delivery error, and
unsold product. Each has a different remedy. Crop loss points to seed,
environment, or sanitation; trim may point to harvest timing; unsold product
points to forecasting or pack size. Set a weekly waste target, review it
without blame, and change one input in the next batch.

## Forecasting from orders

Keep a rolling four-week order forecast by crop and customer. For a crop that
averages 12 sellable ounces per tray, a standing demand of 72 ounces next
week requires six finished trays; if historical crop loss is 15%, plan seven
trays. Convert that harvest need backward through the crop's actual days to
harvest, then place the sowing task on the calendar. Do not use advertised
days to harvest after you have your own data.

| Forecast question | Weekly review action |
|---|---|
| What is already sold? | plant the required base quantity first |
| What sold unexpectedly? | preserve a small fast-crop buffer, not every variety |
| What was discarded? | reduce speculative trays or change pack size |
| Which order was late? | move sowing earlier or revise the promised day |
| What seed reaches reorder point? | order before the next two planned sowings consume it |

# Full 12-Month Business Plan

## Executive summary

Example Microgreens is a home-scale, local fresh-produce venture beginning
with one rack, four core crops, and pre-orders from restaurants, markets, and
subscribers. The goal is not national distribution; it is a dependable local
weekly service that grows from several trays to 20–40 trays per week while
maintaining food-safety records, cold chain, and positive contribution margin.
Figures are planning examples in U.S. dollars, before owner income tax and
debt service. Replace them with local quotes and an adviser-reviewed plan.

## Assumptions and decision rules

The projection assumes that sales are collected promptly, crop losses remain
within the planned allowance, and the operator owns suitable basic space.
It assumes a blended sales price that increases as direct retail and
specialty crops grow, not an automatic increase in crop yield. Review actual
numbers monthly. If revenue is 20% below plan for two months, freeze new
equipment purchases and return to customer interviews. If fill rate falls
below 95%, reduce SKUs or delivery promises until quality recovers. If
contribution margin declines, calculate the cause before discounting.

## Mission and vision

**Mission:** grow clean, flavorful, locally harvested microgreens that make
healthy cooking and chef-quality garnish convenient every week.

**Vision:** become a trusted neighborhood farm brand with documented quality,
low waste, fair customer relationships, and a measured path to a dedicated
production space if recurring demand supports it.

## Unit economics worksheet

Build a price from a real tray record. Suppose a radish tray yields 14
sellable ounces. If seed costs $1.20, medium $0.70, packaging $2.10, tray
wear and sanitation $0.50, and allocated variable labor/delivery $3.90, the
variable cost is $8.40 or $0.60 per ounce. At a $2.00-per-ounce retail price,
the contribution before fixed monthly costs is $19.60 per tray. This is an
illustration, not a required price: use your own actual costs and labor rate.

| Measure | Formula | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Sellable yield | packed ounces ÷ trays | detects declining crop performance |
| Seed efficiency | sellable ounces ÷ seed ounces | compares lots and densities |
| Gross margin | (sales − COGS) ÷ sales | protects against underpricing |
| Average order | sales ÷ number of orders | informs delivery minimums |
| Fill rate | complete orders ÷ orders due | measures reliability |
| Shrink | discarded finished ounces ÷ harvested ounces | reveals forecast/handling loss |

Pay yourself a planned wage in the next version of the model, even if the
first months reinvest cash. A business that only works when owner labor is
free has not found its sustainable scale.

## Break-even analysis

At the mature example mix, variable COGS is roughly 30% of sales, creating a
70% contribution margin. If monthly fixed operating expenses are $600, the
operating break-even sales level is $600 ÷ 0.70 = **about $857 per month**.
To recover an $850 minimum setup too, first-year break-even sales are
approximately ($600 × 12 + $850) / 0.70 = **$11,500**. Recalculate monthly
with actual yield, shrink, price, and delivery mileage. Include an owner wage
before calling the operation economically sustainable.

## Funding and bootstrapping strategy

Start with saved cash, pre-orders, and only equipment that resolves a current
bottleneck. Sell a limited founding subscription or chef trial only after you
can state harvest dates and refund terms. Reinvest a fixed share of actual
net cash into seed inventory, packaging, and one capacity improvement. Avoid
high-interest debt for speculative racks; investigate community microloans,
farm programs, or grants only after confirming eligibility and repayment
obligations.

## Risk factors and mitigation

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Crop loss from mold or poor seed | small lot trials, sanitation logs, airflow, backup seed lots, crop insurance research where relevant |
| Demand falls short | pre-orders, diversified channels, weekly demand review, avoid large fixed leases |
| Food-safety complaint | traceability, cold-chain log, insurance, written response/recall procedure, comply with local rules |
| Equipment/power failure | spare fan/light, temperature alarms, emergency contact plan, avoid overloading circuits |
| Margin erosion | monthly cost and yield review; raise prices or stop unprofitable SKUs promptly |
| Owner burnout | cap delivery days, batch tasks, document routines, add labor only against recurring sales |
